Mobile Product Concept / Sheltr

Research, Product Strategy, and UX Design

Sheltr Safety App UX and Product Design

Sheltr is a safety-focused mobile app concept designed to help people find emergency guidance, understand their rights, access translation support, and connect with trusted contacts during high-stress situations. I led the project's research and UX planning, turning 8 interviews and 31 survey responses into prioritized requirements, information architecture, core user flows, and design direction that helped shape and test the team's high-fidelity prototype.

Overview

A research-backed direction for critical support under pressure

Safety products ask people to interpret important information at moments when time, attention, and trust may already be limited. Sheltr explored how a mobile experience could organize emergency guidance, rights information, translation support, secure document access, and trusted contacts without making the experience feel complicated or alarming.

My work established the product's research and UX foundation. I synthesized qualitative and quantitative research, defined user needs and opportunity areas, developed personas and journey maps, prioritized product requirements, structured the information architecture, and mapped core and edge-case flows. I then contributed to the wireframes, high-fidelity prototype, and usability testing. The result was a clearer, research-backed product direction and a prototype that could be evaluated and improved.

01 Problem Statement

Making critical support easier to navigate under pressure

People may turn to a safety product when they are stressed, uncertain, or short on time. In those moments, confusing navigation, dense language, or unclear privacy expectations can prevent them from finding help. Sheltr needed to bring emergency guidance, rights information, translation support, secure document access, and trusted contacts into one experience without creating additional cognitive burden.

The design challenge was to determine how that information should be organized and prioritized so users could understand their options and move toward an appropriate next step. Because the concept involved sensitive personal information and high-stakes scenarios, clarity, trust, accessibility, and user control needed to shape the product from the beginning.

02 Goals & Success Criteria

Defining success before designing screens

At this stage, success did not mean proving real-world safety outcomes or launching a finished application. It meant creating a testable product direction that made complex support information easier to understand, locate, and act on. The prototype needed to help participants recognize its purpose, navigate high-priority features, understand what would happen next, and feel informed about how sensitive information would be handled.

Evaluation Criteria

  • Comprehension
  • Findability
  • Trust
  • Usability

Create a product direction that could be tested and improved

Success meant participants could interpret key screens, navigate core tasks, and complete important flows with less uncertainty. I evaluated the experience through peer usability testing focused on onboarding, emergency guidance, legal-rights information, translation, and trusted contacts. Participant feedback was then used to revise information architecture, interactions, and content.

Goal 01

Clarity

Labels, hierarchy, and supporting content needed to help participants understand where they were, what each feature provided, and what action they could take next.

Goal 02

Trust and Control

The experience needed to communicate the purpose of sensitive features clearly, avoid unsupported promises, and help users understand when and how their personal information could be used.

Goal 03

Findability

Emergency guidance, rights information, translation, and trusted contacts needed to remain easy to locate through consistent navigation and clearly grouped content.

03 Research & Discovery

Turning early research into product priorities

Research began with 8 interviews and a survey that received 31 responses. The goal was to understand what people expected from a safety-focused product, which capabilities they considered valuable, and what concerns could prevent them from trusting or using it.

Data security emerged as a clear priority, selected by 24 respondents, while 23 identified secure document access as a valuable capability. These findings did not validate a finished product, but they provided evidence for prioritizing transparency, user control, and clear explanations around sensitive information. Combined with interview findings and competitive research, the work produced 3 personas and 30 documented UX opportunities that guided feature prioritization, information architecture, and core user flows.

What the research changed

Key Insights

  • Data security was a central concern, with 24 of 31 respondents identifying it as a priority.
  • Secure document access showed meaningful interest, with 23 respondents selecting it as valuable.
  • Emergency guidance, rights information, and translation support needed clear separation so users could identify the appropriate path quickly.
  • The number of potential features made prioritization essential. The experience could not present every capability with equal weight or urgency.

How the direction was established

Methods

  • Conducted 8 user interviews and collected 31 survey responses.
  • Synthesized findings through affinity mapping, empathy maps, journey maps, and 3 personas.
  • Reviewed comparable products and interaction patterns related to safety, privacy, emergency support, and trusted contacts.
  • Documented 30 UX opportunities, then used feature prioritization to translate the research into product requirements and core flows.

04 Key Decisions

Turning research into practical product rules

The most important decisions were structural, not decorative. Based on the research, I focused on how information was grouped, which actions received priority, when additional explanation was needed, and how much control users retained.

The prototype's visual direction continued to evolve, but these UX rules gave the team a clearer foundation for organizing and evaluating the experience.

Decision 01

Prioritize by Urgency, Not Feature Count

Emergency guidance, rights information, translation support, secure documents, and trusted contacts served different needs and could not be treated as interchangeable features. I organized them into distinct pathways so higher-priority actions could remain easier to identify without forcing every capability into the same level of prominence.

Product RuleOrganize the experience around the urgency of the user's need.

Decision 02

Make Privacy Part of the Interaction

With 24 of 31 survey respondents prioritizing data security, privacy could not be hidden inside background language. Sensitive flows needed to explain why information was requested, what could be shared, and when users retained control. This was especially important for document access and trusted-contact features.

Product RuleExplain purpose, sharing, and control where sensitive actions happen.

Decision 03

Reduce Cognitive Load Through Consistency

Plain language, readable hierarchy, predictable controls, and consistent navigation patterns helped make the concept easier to interpret. Accessibility considerations were treated as part of the core safety experience because users under stress should not have to relearn the interface while trying to understand what to do next.

Product RuleMake the next step understandable without requiring users to relearn the interface.

05 Validation & Iteration

Testing the product direction before treating it as complete

Once the core flows and high-fidelity prototype were developed, I used peer usability testing to evaluate whether participants could understand the product, locate important information, and move through key tasks without unnecessary confusion.

Testing covered onboarding, emergency guidance, legal-rights information, translation support, and trusted contacts. The purpose was not to certify the concept as ready for launch. It was to identify where the structure, interactions, or content created hesitation and give the team evidence for another round of decisions.

What We Evaluated

  • Whether participants understood Sheltr's purpose during onboarding
  • How easily participants could locate emergency and support resources
  • Whether legal-rights and translation information felt understandable and appropriately organized
  • How participants interpreted trusted-contact features and sensitive information

How Feedback Was Used

Participant feedback informed revisions to labels, interaction sequences, supporting content, and information hierarchy. Areas that caused uncertainty were simplified or clarified, helping the prototype communicate its purpose and available actions more consistently.

What remained outside the tested concept

What Still Required Validation

The concept would have needed broader testing with more representative participants, realistic high-stress scenarios, a formal accessibility review, privacy and security evaluation, and technical feasibility work before being considered ready for development or launch.

Project Boundary

A collaborative concept with a clearly defined contribution

Sheltr was a collaborative, early-stage product concept. My contribution centered on research, product planning, information architecture, user flows, and usability testing. The visual interface was developed collaboratively and is not presented as my individual design work.

06 Conclusion & Reflection

A stronger research foundation and a more testable product direction

What Worked

Research became a decision-making tool

The research created a stronger foundation than assumptions alone could have provided. Eight interviews and 31 survey responses were translated into 3 personas, 30 documented UX opportunities, prioritized requirements, information architecture, and testable user flows. That work gave the team clearer evidence for deciding what the product needed to support and how its features should be organized.

What I Would Refine

Narrow the product earlier

With my current experience, I would define a narrower minimum viable product earlier, separate essential safety functions from future feature ideas, and begin usability testing before the high-fidelity stage. I would also recruit participants beyond the immediate peer network and include formal accessibility, privacy, security, and technical-feasibility reviews before moving toward development.

What I Learned

Research only matters when it changes the product

Sheltr was my first substantial UX product project, and it taught me that completing research artifacts is not the same as making research useful. Interviews, surveys, personas, and journey maps only create value when they influence priorities, structure, interactions, and testing. The project also strengthened my understanding that safety-focused products require restraint, plain language, user control, and continued validation rather than unnecessary complexity.
